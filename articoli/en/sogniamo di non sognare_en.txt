titolo: We Dream of Not Dreaming
pubblicazione: 2026-10-13
testo:
We leave the scenic route of the Florence–Bologna road for lunch in the August heat. Lunch, I confess, was excellent — a rare satisfaction these days (especially if you live in Marseille).

After lunch you go in to pay: the interior has the vaults of an old cellar. A little old lady is making pasta, surrounded by tagliatelle — authentic Italianness.

The scene is the holy grail of every American visiting Italy. My first reaction is to think, "how touristy." My instinct is to reject what I see (even though I'm in a fairly out-of-the-way trattoria).

It might sound crazy, but think about:
1. Bari Vecchia, with little old ladies making orecchiette as set dressing;
2. the ladies in Bologna and Rome put on display in restaurants for years now (https://www.ilpost.it/2026/07/24/pasta-spettacolarizzazione/);
3. the Miscusi chain.

So, I have excellent reasons to be suspicious of what I'm seeing. But that's exactly the point. What am I supposed to suspect? What am I afraid of?

I've already written that the authentic is the opposite of the real. The authentic is the identification of the real with the fantasized, and as such it is "artificial." No one would have called that grandmother authentic sixty years ago, because she was simply real. This tells us a lot. For instance, that when we read "authentic" the pull is so strong precisely because it doesn't mean "real." Who would want to eat a real Italian pizza? Maybe the pizza maker is tired that day. It means instead "just like in your fantasies," and then it's a whole different matter. But perhaps even more interesting is understanding where this obsession with the authentic comes from.

It seems to me we've reached an inversion of the nightmare paradigm. In horror chapter books for kids, the protagonist goes through terrible things only to wake up and say, "thank god it was just a dream."

Now, instead, we see wonderful, perfect things, but we know they're dreams. We go looking for others, hoping some imperfection will prove their reality, but they're still dreams. So we seek out popular, even ugly places, but they're still dreams; we buy torn t-shirts, we go to underground venues, we travel to poor places, but they're still dreams. The nightmare is that we're inside a dream. And we can't get out of this nightmare because, instead of simply waking up, we just keep switching dreams.

It's obvious: we're terrified because we know nothing is real anymore, that everything is narrative, spectacle. But it's our response that proves how screwed we are. We respond with a frantic search for origins, purity, naturalness. We change the show instead of stopping being spectators (and extras: by now we're always both).

If it didn't mean something else, we'd have to call this response to the crisis of reality we live in an identity obsession. We're all searching for the lost identities of things: some for authentic food, others for organic (that is, non-artisanal) materials, others for lost masculinity, others for a time when sexuality was free and unconditioned by society, others for a place uncontaminated by tourists, others for their own self. Everyone wants an identity, or something that has one.

But it's precisely this identity-driven search for something that isn't a dream that is the ultimate spectacle, the ultimate erotic fantasy, the biggest dream of all. We're in a nightmare, and we dream of not dreaming.
